# Interview 003: Sarah Williams

**Date**: January 18, 2024  
**Duration**: 47 minutes  
**Role**: Founder/Service Provider  
**Business**: Virtual Assistant Services  
**Business Model**: Recurring client support (10-15 hours/week per client)  
**Company Size**: Solo  
**Annual Revenue**: ~$72K  
**Years in Business**: 3  
**Current Tools**: Google Spreadsheet, PayPal, Gmail  
**Ledger Status**: Non-user (uses manual tracking)

---

## Background & Context

Sarah started as a VA 3 years ago and now has 8 recurring clients she supports weekly. Revenue is relatively stable ($6K/month), but highly fragmented—each client has different payment arrangements and frequencies. She works from her home in Portland and deliberately keeps the business small to maintain work-life balance.

Sarah is relationship-focused. She values client relationships and is sensitive to how she communicates about payment. This is central to her problem.

---

## The Problem: Emotional Barrier to Invoicing

### The Core Issue

**Sarah's own words**: "I hate sending invoices for recurring clients because it feels like I'm pestering them. Like, I helped you last week, and now I'm asking for money. It feels icky."

This is not a functional problem. Sarah *could* send invoices weekly. But something prevents her emotionally.

### How She Currently Works

**Ideal workflow** (what she knows she should do):
1. Track hours worked (rough estimate in her head)
2. Generate invoice every week
3. Send to client
4. Receive payment
5. Record in spreadsheet

**Actual workflow** (what she does):
1. Works for clients throughout the week
2. Thinks "I should invoice" but feels awkward
3. Waits 2-3 weeks
4. Finally sends invoice, apologizing: "Hey, here's what I've done for you..."
5. Client says "Oh, invoice me whenever"
6. Sarah feels permission to invoice irregularly

**Result**: Sarah bills on average **every 3-4 weeks** instead of every 1-2 weeks.

### The Financial Impact

**Conservative estimate**:
- Should be billing: 40-50 hours/month × $30-40/hr = $1,200-2,000/month
- Actually bills: ~$1,500/month (because of irregular invoicing)
- **Lost revenue**: ~$500/month or $6,000/year

She's leaving 5-10% of revenue on the table due to infrequent invoicing.

---

## The Emotional Roots

**Researcher asked**: "What is it about invoicing that feels icky?"

**Sarah**: "Like, I'm immediately following good work with asking for money. It feels like I'm being transactional. Professional, sure, but also... mercenary? Like I only care about the money?"

**Researcher**: "Have any of your clients complained about your invoicing?"

**Sarah**: "Not at all. They actually appreciate when I invoice because they can track what I've done. But *I* feel weird about it."

This is pure psychology. No client has complained. But Sarah's internal shame about "asking for money" prevents her from invoicing as frequently as she should.

### The Shame Component

**Sarah**: "Money conversations feel awkward. If I invoice weekly, it's like I'm constantly asking. It's embarrassing. But if it comes from the system, not from me personally, it's fine."

Key insight: **Automation removes the personal element.**

If a system sends "Invoice from VA Services" it's professional. If Sarah manually sends "Here's my bill," it feels needy.

---

## Current Workaround

**Tracking system**: Google Sheet with columns:
- Client name
- Date worked
- Hours (estimated)
- Rate
- Total
- Invoice sent? (yes/no)
- Payment received? (yes/no)

**Updates**: Sporadic. Sarah updates it when she thinks of it, often only when a payment arrives.

**Pain points**:
- No automated reminders to invoice
- No visibility into which clients owe what
- Sometimes forgets to invoice a client for 4+ weeks
- No payment tracking (relies on PayPal email notifications)

**Sarah**: "I don't really track what's outstanding. If I needed cash, I'd email clients asking if they want to pay me, which is awkward."

---

## What Would Change Her Behavior

**Sarah explicitly said**: "If the system just handled it automatically, and I didn't have to think about it or feel weird about it, I'd do it weekly instead of quarterly."

### What "Automatic" Means to Sarah

1. **Recurring invoice template**: "Can you set up weekly invoicing for each client automatically? So I just log hours, and it invoices automatically?"

2. **Professional sender**: "Instead of my name, it comes from 'Sarah's VA Services' or just 'Invoice.' Not my personal email. It feels less personal that way."

3. **No follow-up needed from me**: "Client gets invoice. They pay. I don't have to think about it."

---

## Jobs to Be Done

### Primary Job
**Bill recurring clients on a predictable schedule without feeling like I'm pestering them**

Steps:
1. Complete work for client
2. System automatically invoices (weekly or bi-weekly, per client preference)
3. Client pays (ideally automatically)
4. Sarah records payment

**Emotional job**: Feel professional and not exploitative; remove shame from payment conversation

### Secondary Job
**Understand which clients pay on time vs. late**

Sarah wants to know: "Client A always pays day 2. Client B takes 10 days. Client C is always late." This would help her forecast cash.

---

## Specific Needs

1. **Recurring invoice templates** - Set up once, auto-invoice every week/2 weeks
2. **Auto-billing option** - Can clients authorize recurring charges (ACH/credit card)?
3. **Payment tracking** - Show what's unpaid and how many days overdue
4. **Reminder removal** - Sarah doesn't want to send reminders; she prefers automation
5. **Simple dashboard** - "How much do I have outstanding today?"

## Segment-Specific Insight: Recurring Revenue

**Key finding**: Solopreneurs with recurring clients have a unique problem—they get comfortable with clients and stop invoicing frequently.

**Sarah's dynamic**: 
- Client says "Invoice me whenever"
- Sarah interprets this as "invoice when you feel like it"
- Client actually expects weekly invoicing, but never says anything
- Sarah feels permission to invoice infrequently

**Solution**: Auto-billing removes this dynamic. Clients authorize it upfront; Sarah doesn't have to think about it.

---

## What Sarah Still Wants

1. **Credit card auto-billing**: "Can clients authorize recurring charges so I don't have to chase payment?"
2. **Expense tracking**: "I have client-specific expenses sometimes. Can I bundle those into the invoice?"
3. **Project-based work handling**: "Some clients also hire me for projects (not recurring). Can I mix both?"
4. **Client portal**: "Can clients see their invoice history and payment history without me managing it?"

---

## Behavioral Evidence

**Sarah is likely to adopt a solution that**:
- Requires minimal setup (she's not technical)
- Automatically sends invoices
- Removes her from the payment conversation
- Provides simple payment tracking

**Sarah is unlikely to adopt**:
- Complex accounting software (feels too formal)
- Invoicing tools that require manual sending (defeats the purpose)
- Tools that make her feel like she's "nickel-and-diming" clients

## Product Implications

**For Ledger**: Sarah represents a significant market—solopreneurs with recurring clients. Her pain point (emotional, not functional) is different from Raj's (operational). 

The solution:
- For Raj: Dashboard + visibility
- For Sarah: Automation + invisibility

Build the product to handle both.
